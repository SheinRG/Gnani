1 — What the assignment actually is

Strip away the jargon and it is this:

Someone gives your website a recording of a person talking. Your website gives back the words that were said, plus a short summary of them. And it remembers everything they have uploaded before.

Three deliverables to the user: a transcript, a summary, a history. Everything else in the plan exists to make those three things work reliably.

The brief also names four hard requirements:

  • It must be deployed — a URL that works with no setup on the reviewer's machine.
  • It must have an /architecture page explaining the system, linking the GitHub repo.
  • It must handle failures visibly — upload errors, API timeouts, corrupt files.
  • It must show upload and processing progress.

2 — Why Gnani set this assignment

ASR = Automatic Speech Recognition. Software that listens to audio and writes down the words. Also called STT (Speech-To-Text) — same thing, two names. Gnani's product is ASR, specifically for Indian languages. That is their entire company.

Now look at these two facts side by side.

The brief saysGnani's API docs say
"User uploads an audio file (2+ min)" Maximum duration: 60 seconds (ideal ≤ 30s)
This is not an accident. The assignment is impossible if you use their API the obvious way. They deliberately asked for something their own synchronous endpoint cannot do in a single call. The real question being asked is: when you hit a wall, do you notice it, and what do you build?

Everything else on their list — progress bars, error handling, the architecture page — checks whether you can build a system rather than a script. A script calls an API. A system handles the file being 12 minutes long, the API being down, the user closing the tab, and the file being a corrupted MP3 that is actually a renamed PDF.

3 — The real-life scenario, end to end

Meet Priya. She is a sales rep. She has just finished a 7-minute call with a client and recorded it on her phone. She wants notes without listening again. Here is every single thing that happens from the moment she opens your site.

0 The two computers

There are always two computers in a web app.

  • The client — Priya's phone, running your React code inside her browser. She can see it. She can open DevTools and read every line. Never put a secret here.
  • The server — a computer in a data centre running your backend code. She cannot see inside it. Your Gnani API key lives here and only here.
Why this matters practically: if you called Gnani's API from the browser, your API key would be visible to anyone who opens DevTools, and they would spend your Rs. 1,000 of credits. So every call to Gnani goes from your server, never from the browser.

1 She drops the file in

She picks English (India) from the dropdown and drags in call_recording.m4a — 7 minutes, 6 MB.

Why a language dropdown?

Gnani's API has a required parameter called language_code. It does not guess. You must tell it "this audio is Hindi" or "this audio is Tamil". The codes look like hi-IN, ta-IN, en-IN — that format is a standard called BCP-47. hi is the language, IN is the region. So en-IN means "English as spoken in India", which is a genuinely different acoustic model from American English — trained on Indian accents.

2 Storage, and why there are two kinds

Your app stores two completely different kinds of thing.

Vercel Blob (object storage) Neon Postgres (database)
Stores Big files — the actual audio bytes Small structured facts — "note 47 is 7 min long, status = processing"
Good at Holding megabytes cheaply Answering questions like "list Priya's notes, newest first"
Bad at Being queried Holding megabytes

Blob literally means Binary Large OBject. Object storage is a hard drive on the internet: you hand it a file, it hands you back a URL. It cannot answer "which files are longer than 5 minutes" — that is what the database is for.

So: the audio goes in Blob, the facts about the audio go in Postgres. The database stores the Blob's URL as a text column, linking the two.

The upload itself

Priya's browser sends the file directly to Blob. Your server only issues a short-lived permission slip (a "client token") saying "this browser may upload one file". The 6 MB never touches your server.

Two reasons for doing it that way:

  1. Your server is not tied up for 40 seconds shovelling bytes.
  2. The browser upload API gives you an onUploadProgress event that fires as bytes leave the phone. That is how you draw a real progress bar. The standard fetch() function gives you nothing while uploading — you would have no idea whether it was at 5% or 95%.

3 The job is created

Upload finishes. The browser tells your server: "done, here is the Blob URL, language is en-IN."

Your server writes a row to the database:

notes: id=47, status='pending', language='en-IN', blob_url='https://...'

…and immediately replies 202 Accepted with { id: 47 }.

HTTP status codes you should know cold

CodeMeansWhere you will see it
200 OKWorkedA successful transcript
202 Accepted"I have taken your request, I will work on it"Your job creation endpoint
400 Bad RequestYou sent something invalidGnani rejects a bad audio format
401 UnauthorizedBad or missing API keyWrong X-API-Key-ID
429 Too Many RequestsYou are going too fast, slow downGnani rate-limiting your fan-out
500 Internal Server ErrorTheir code brokeA Gnani hiccup
503 Service UnavailableThey are down or overloadedGnani maintenance

Why 202 and not 200?

Because transcribing 7 minutes of audio takes roughly 60 seconds, and an HTTP request that hangs for 60 seconds will be killed by browsers, proxies and load balancers. So you do not make her wait on the request. You say "got it, job 47", close the connection, and do the work separately.

TermMeaningAnalogy
SynchronousAsk, and wait on the line for the answerA phone call
AsynchronousAsk, hang up, get told laterSending a text

Gnani's /stt/v3 endpoint is synchronous. Your job endpoint is asynchronous.

4 Validation, before you spend a rupee

The job starts. The first thing it does — before touching Gnani — is run ffprobe.

ffmpeg is the universal audio and video tool. It converts, cuts, merges and analyses almost any media format. It is a command-line program — you run it with arguments, not by importing a library. ffprobe is its inspector twin: it changes nothing, it only reports what a file is.

You run ffprobe and it reports: duration 7m 12s, codec AAC, 44.1 kHz, stereo. That gives you three things:

  1. Corrupt-file detection. If someone renames invoice.pdf to audio.mp3, ffprobe fails immediately with "Invalid data found when processing input". You fail the job in 200 ms with a clear message, instead of sending garbage to Gnani and burning credits on 14 failed calls.
  2. True duration. The browser lies about this or does not know. ffprobe is authoritative.
  3. Enforcing the 10-minute cap. A 45-minute podcast gets rejected here, cheaply, before any API spend.
This is called a pre-flight check — validate before committing to expensive work. Same idea as checking you have your passport before driving to the airport.

5 Chunking — the heart of the whole thing

7m 12s = 432 seconds. Gnani accepts 60. You need pieces.

The naive approach, and why it is bad

Cut every 30 seconds: ffmpeg -f segment -segment_time 30. Fifteen pieces, one command. But at exactly 30.000 seconds Priya was mid-sentence:

"...so the revenue pro | jection for Q3 looks strong..."

The word projection is sliced in half. The ASR hears half a word in each chunk and produces garbage at every seam. In a 7-minute file that is 14 damaged spots, and a reviewer running one demo will see all of them.

Silence-aware splitting

Run ffmpeg's silencedetect filter first. It scans the audio and reports every moment quieter than a threshold for longer than, say, 0.3 seconds — every natural pause between sentences:

silence_start: 24.8   silence_end: 25.4
silence_start: 51.2   silence_end: 51.9
...

Then instead of cutting blindly at 25 s, you cut at the nearest silence to the 25 s mark — 24.8 s here. Nobody was speaking. No word is damaged. If there is no silence within ±5 s (someone talking non-stop), you cut at 30 s anyway, because 30 is the hard ceiling.

This costs one extra ffmpeg pass and some text parsing. It is the single most technically interesting thing in the entire project, and it is exactly what "long-audio handling" means on the architecture page.

Result: 15 chunks, roughly 25–29 s each, each cut at a pause. You write 15 rows to the chunks table:

chunks: note_id=47, idx=0, start_ms=0,     end_ms=24800, status='pending'
        note_id=47, idx=1, start_ms=24800, end_ms=51200, status='pending'
        ...

You keep idx because the pieces must be reassembled in order — chunk 7's text has to land between 6 and 8. You keep start_ms and end_ms because if chunk 7 fails, you can tell the user exactly which 26 seconds of their recording are missing.

6 Fan-out, concurrency and retries

Fan-out

Fan-out = taking one task and splitting it into many parallel sub-tasks. Picture one arrow coming in and fifteen arrows going out. The reverse — collecting the fifteen results back into one — is called fan-in.

The alternative is sequential: chunk 0, wait, chunk 1, wait, chunk 2… At roughly 3 seconds each that is 45 seconds. Fan-out does them at the same time.

Concurrency

Concurrency = how many things you allow to be in progress simultaneously.

"Concurrency 4" means at most 4 requests to Gnani are in flight at any moment. When one finishes, the next chunk starts. Like a shop with 4 checkout counters and 15 customers — 4 are being served, the rest wait in line, and it stays exactly 4 until the queue empties.

QuestionAnswer
Why not fire all 15 at once? Gnani sends back 429 Too Many Requests. Their docs list 429 as a real error but publish no actual rate limit, so you are flying blind — start conservative.
Why not 1 (sequential)? 45 seconds of staring at a screen versus ~12 seconds is a real product difference.

Net effect: 15 chunks, 4 at a time, roughly 12 seconds total.

Retries and exponential backoff

Networks fail. APIs hiccup. Chunk 7 comes back 500.

Retry = try again. "3 retries" = try up to 3 more times before giving up.

But when do you retry? If you retry instantly and the reason for failure was that Gnani is overloaded, you have just added more load to an overloaded server. So you wait, and you wait longer each time:

AttemptWait before it
1st retry1 second
2nd retry2 seconds
3rd retry4 seconds

Each wait is double the last. That doubling is why it is called exponential backoff. It gives a struggling service room to recover instead of stampeding it.

In production you would also add jitter — a small random offset — so that if 100 clients all fail at once they do not all retry at the same instant. Worth knowing the word; overkill for this project.

Which errors do you retry?

ErrorActionWhy
429 / 500 / 503RetryTransient — it might work in a second
400 Bad RequestDo not retryPermanent — the audio format is wrong and will be wrong forever. Retrying wastes 3 calls to learn nothing.
Knowing the difference between a transient failure and a permanent one is the whole skill here. Retry the first kind, surface the second immediately.

After the retries are exhausted

Chunk 7 has failed 4 times. Two philosophies:

  • All-or-nothing: the note failed. Throw away the 14 successful transcriptions and the credits spent on them.
  • Graceful degradation: keep the 14, insert a marker where 7 should have been, tell the user clearly.

We chose graceful degradation. Priya's transcript reads:

...discussed the migration timeline.
[transcription unavailable: 3:04-3:31]
...and she confirmed the budget was approved.

The note is marked completed_with_errors, a banner says "1 of 15 segments failed", and a Retry failed segments button re-runs only chunk 7 — which is cheap, because the chunks table knows exactly which one broke and where it starts.

She got 93% of her value instead of 0%. That is what "handle failures visibly" means: the failure is shown, not hidden, and not fatal.

7 Stitching

All chunks done. Fetch them ordered by idx, join the text, and you have the full transcript. This is the fan-in.

8 The summary

The transcript (~1,100 words for 7 minutes) goes to an LLM (Large Language Model) — ChatGPT-style AI. Gnani does not sell one; they do speech, not text generation. So this comes from elsewhere.

You send a prompt — an instruction plus the transcript — and ask for structured output. That last part matters. If you just ask "summarise this", you get a paragraph of prose you would have to parse with string manipulation. Instead you define a schema (using Zod, a TypeScript library for describing data shapes):

{ title: string, tldr: string, keyPoints: string[], actionItems: string[] }

and the AI SDK's generateObject() guarantees you get back exactly that shape, validated, or it throws. No parsing, no "sometimes it wrapped the answer in markdown".

The title is the sneaky-valuable field. Without it, Priya's history page is a list of WhatsApp Audio 2026-08-24 at 14.32.m4a. With it, it is "Acme Corp — Q3 renewal discussion". That one field is the difference between a demo and a product.

Vercel AI Gateway is a middleman: instead of integrating with Anthropic and OpenAI and Google separately, you send everything to the Gateway with a model name like "anthropic/claude-sonnet-4-5" and it routes for you. One API key instead of three, plus model fallback — if the primary model is down it automatically tries a backup. That fallback is a direct, honest answer to the brief's "API timeouts" requirement.

9 Meanwhile — what has Priya been seeing?

This whole time, roughly 75 seconds, Priya watches a progress display. How does it know?

Polling. Her browser asks your server "what is the status of job 47?" every 2 seconds. Your server reads the database and answers. Ask, wait 2 s, ask again. Like refreshing a package tracking page, automatically.

GET /api/jobs/47  →  { status: 'transcribing', done: 7, total: 15 }
OptionHow it worksWhy not here
Polling (chosen) Client asks repeatedly Simple, survives a page refresh, works everywhere
SSE (Server-Sent Events) Server holds a connection open and pushes updates The work runs in a different server invocation, so an SSE endpoint would have to poll the database internally anyway — same polling, more complexity
WebSockets Two-way persistent connection Massive overkill — you have no two-way traffic

Because progress lives in Postgres and not in browser memory, Priya can close her laptop, reopen it, and the page picks up at "Transcribing 11/15". That is a real feature you get free from putting state in the database.

What she actually sees — the state machine

Uploading 62%  →  Validating audio  →  Splitting into 15 segments
               →  Transcribing 7/15  →  Summarizing  →  Done

Every one of those numbers is measured, not animated. 7/15 is a literal COUNT(*) WHERE status='done' from your chunks table. You earned that number by chunking — a candidate who sent one big file to a batch API can only show a spinner.

10 What if the server dies mid-job?

Serverless functions can be killed. Deploys happen. Things crash. If your job dies at chunk 9, note 47 sits at status='processing' forever and Priya's page spins until she gives up.

So: a stale-job reaper. A cron job (a task that runs automatically on a schedule) fires every 5 minutes and runs one query — "any note still processing that started more than 15 minutes ago?" — and marks those failed with "processing timed out".

Small piece of code, big signal. It is the difference between "I built the happy path" and "I thought about what happens when it breaks."

11 The history

Priya returns tomorrow. How does your app know which notes are hers, with no login?

A cookie — a small piece of data the server asks the browser to store and send back on every future request. On her first visit you generate a random ID and set it as an httpOnly cookie (httpOnly means JavaScript on the page cannot read it, only the server can — this protects against certain attacks). Every note she creates is tagged with that ID. Her list is SELECT * FROM notes WHERE session_id = <her cookie>.

No signup, no password, and the reviewer can use the app in 3 seconds. The honest limitation — clear your cookies and your notes are orphaned — goes on the architecture page.

4 — ASR concepts specific to this company

Things that will come up if you work at an ASR company.

ConceptWhat it means
Sample rate Audio is captured by measuring air pressure many times per second. 16,000 times/sec = 16 kHz. Phone calls are 8 kHz, music is 44.1 kHz. Gnani auto-converts everything to 16 kHz mono — the standard for speech models, because human speech lives below 8 kHz and higher rates add data without adding information.
Mono vs stereo One channel vs two. Speech models want mono; stereo gets averaged down.
ITN
Inverse Text Normalization		 Converting spoken forms to written forms: "twenty five" → "25", "january third" → "Jan 3". Gnani's format parameter controls this — verbatim writes exactly what was said including "um"; transcribe enables ITN. For notes and summaries, ITN reads far better. Core ASR vocabulary.
Diarization Figuring out who spoke when — "Speaker 1 said this, Speaker 2 said that". Gnani's /stt/v3 response has no diarization; it returns a flat string. That is why speaker labels are impossible in this project and belong in "what I would improve", rather than having the LLM invent them.
WER
Word Error Rate		 The standard accuracy metric for ASR — what percentage of words were wrong. This is the number ASR companies compete on. Knowing this term signals you understand their industry.
Code-switching Mixing languages mid-sentence — "मैंने usko call किया but he didn't pick up." Extremely common in India and genuinely hard for ASR. A big part of why Gnani exists as an Indian ASR company rather than everyone just using an off-the-shelf model.

6 — Assumptions baked into the build

  • ffprobe pre-flight is job step 0; nothing reaches ASR before the file is proven decodable.
  • ASR fan-out concurrency 4, 3 retries, exponential backoff on 429/500/503.
  • Client polls /api/jobs/:id every 2 s (not SSE — with waitUntil an SSE route would just poll Postgres for you).
  • Summarization is one call over the full stitched transcript; map-reduce is documented as future work, not built.
  • Note detail page shows an <audio> player against the Blob URL alongside transcript and summary.
  • No test suite — four days, and nothing in the brief asks for one.
  • Gap markers render inline as [transcription unavailable: 3:04–3:31].
  • No subagents. One coherent codebase, written and interrogated in one session, so the code is genuinely owned.

9 — Interview questions to rehearse

If you can answer these out loud without notes, you own this project.

  1. Gnani's API takes 60 seconds. The brief asked for 2+ minutes. What did you do, and why that way?
  2. Why cut on silence instead of every 30 seconds? What breaks if you do not?
  3. Why concurrency 4? What happens at 15? What happens at 1?
  4. Chunk 7 of 15 fails. Walk me through exactly what the user sees and what the database looks like.
  5. Which HTTP errors do you retry and which do you not? Why?
  6. Why exponential backoff rather than retrying immediately?
  7. Why does your job endpoint return 202 instead of the transcript?
  8. What runs synchronously and what runs in the background? Draw the boundary.
  9. The server crashes at chunk 9. What happens to that note? Who notices, and how?
  10. Why is the audio in Blob and not in Postgres?
  11. Why does the upload go browser → Blob instead of browser → your server → Blob?
  12. Why polling instead of WebSockets or SSE?
  13. Where does your Gnani API key live, and why can it never be in the client?
  14. A user uploads a renamed PDF. Trace what happens, step by step.
  15. How would you raise the 10-minute cap to 2 hours? What would have to change first?
  16. What are the three weakest parts of your system, and what would you build instead?
